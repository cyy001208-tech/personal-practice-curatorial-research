# Curatorial Methods for Personal-Practice Research

Use these models as operations, not decorative references. Select only the methods that illuminate the archive and state each method's blind spot.

## Individual mythology and the archipelago

**Source:** [documenta 5: Questioning Reality—Visual Worlds Today](https://documenta.de/en/retrospective/documenta-5)

documenta 5 placed “individual mythologies,” self-representation, process, everyday imagery, advertising, commodity aesthetics, and other visual worlds in proximity. The curatorial effect came from relations among heterogeneous materials rather than a single hierarchy of “major works.”

Apply by:

- tracing recurring motifs, rituals, references, and working habits;
- placing final outputs beside drafts, mundane sources, refusals, and peripheral artifacts;
- asking what becomes visible only through juxtaposition.

Guard against:

- romanticizing repetition as destiny;
- turning coincidence into a fixed personal essence;
- letting the curator's vocabulary replace the practitioner's evidence.

## Platforms

**Source:** [Documenta11](https://documenta.de/en/retrospective/documenta11)

Documenta11 treated the Kassel exhibition as the fifth platform after distributed, transdisciplinary research and debate. The visible exhibition was therefore one manifestation of a larger knowledge process.

Apply by separating:

1. archive formation;
2. project reconstruction;
3. oral history;
4. contextual and comparative research;
5. public portfolio/exhibition.

Guard against treating the final portfolio as a neutral container or allowing the display phase to rewrite the research record.

## Leitmotifs

**Source:** [documenta 12](https://documenta.de/en/retrospective/documenta-12)

documenta 12 used three recurring questions to structure a heterogeneous exhibition. Questions created continuity without forcing every work into the same category.

Apply by:

- deriving three questions from repeated tensions in the archive;
- testing every project against the questions;
- allowing a project to answer, complicate, or refuse a question.
- converting evidence-backed answers into Key Messages during final synthesis;
- retaining only genuinely unresolved questions under the final Critical Questions heading.

Guard against vague questions that can absorb any project and therefore explain nothing.

## Brain and research notes

**Sources:** [dOCUMENTA (13)](https://documenta.de/en/retrospective/documenta-13) and [100 Notes–100 Thoughts](https://documenta.de/en/publications/100-notes-100-thoughts-100-notizen-100-gedanken)

dOCUMENTA (13) concentrated heterogeneous objects and documents in a “Brain,” while its notebook series made research fragments and interdisciplinary thought visible before the exhibition.

Apply by:

- selecting 10–20 decisive artifacts from across projects;
- building a compact nucleus before presenting complete cases;
- treating notes, sketches, rejected plans, and minor objects as potential evidence;
- using unexpected adjacency to generate a hypothesis, then verifying it against full dossiers.

Guard against aestheticizing fragments or presenting opaque associations as proof.

## Dual perspective and situated learning

**Source:** [documenta 14: Learning from Athens](https://documenta.de/en/retrospective/documenta-14)

documenta 14 worked across Athens and Kassel to challenge explanation from one location and position. For a personal case study, no account should rely entirely on self-description or entirely on an external viewer.

Apply by comparing:

- self-account and project record;
- intended role and collaborator evidence;
- original context and current interpretation;
- private meaning and audience reading.

Guard against treating two perspectives as automatically equal when their evidence quality differs.

## Lumbung and relational authorship

**Source:** [documenta fifteen](https://documenta.de/en/retrospective/documenta-fifteen)

documenta fifteen treated lumbung as an operational practice of shared resources, knowledge, decision-making, and long-term reciprocity, not simply a theme.

Apply by mapping:

- resources contributed and received;
- individual and collective decisions;
- invisible care, coordination, and infrastructure;
- what the practitioner enables other people to do;
- which outcomes belong to a network rather than one author.

Guard against erasing individual responsibility inside a collective narrative or claiming collective output as personal authorship.

## Archive as displayed argument

**Sources:** [MoMA on thematic archive displays](https://www.moma.org/explore/inside_out/2014/04/07/once-upon-a-time-archives-tales-at-the-van-abbemuseum/), [MoMA Oral History](https://www.moma.org/research/archives/oral-history)

Archive materials can carry the exhibition's argument rather than merely decorate a finished work. Oral history can recover missing firsthand knowledge, but testimony remains situated and must be compared with records.

Apply by assigning every displayed archive item a narrative job and by using specific interview questions to fill documented gaps.

Guard against using archival quantity as a substitute for relevance or memory as an uncontested chronology.

## Interpretive hierarchy and audience

**Sources:** [Smithsonian Guide to Exhibit Development](https://exhibits.si.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Guide-to-Exhibit-Development.pdf), [V&A Principles of Exhibition Making](https://www.vam.ac.uk/event/8nWBNnvg9O/o25018-principles-of-exhibition-making)

Smithsonian exhibition planning distinguishes a Big Idea, key messages, and critical questions. V&A frames exhibition making through mission, audience, research, experience design, project management, collaboration, and a curatorial voice capable of carrying multiple perspectives. This skill sharpens those categories further through the local [Interpretive Role Contract](interpretive-role-contract.md): Critical Questions are reserved for what remains unresolved, Theme Constellations hold evidence relations, and Portfolio Chapters control reading order.

Apply by:

- fixing the intended audience and desired understanding;
- generating and testing two or three competing person interpretations before writing one contestable Big Idea;
- recording whether those interpretations are selected, merged, retained as coexisting identities, or left unresolved;
- limiting Key Messages to two through four distinct audience judgments;
- naming one to three unresolved Critical Questions;
- grouping supporting projects and materials into Theme Constellations;
- translating only established conclusions into ordered Portfolio Chapters;
- rejecting attractive material that does not serve the case.

Guard against reducing a complex person to a slogan. The Big Idea is an interpretive proposition derived from a documented comparison, not a permanent identity or the first attractive phrase.
