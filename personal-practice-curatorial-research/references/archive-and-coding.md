# Archive Formation and Cross-Project Coding

## 1. Build a source hierarchy

Distinguish source role from file format:

Source role | Examples | Typical evidentiary use
--- | --- | ---
Finished object (`O`) | final design, published output, shipped artifact | what became public or operational
Process (`P`) | sketch, rejected draft, plan, decision log, prototype | how choices and methods developed
Testimony (`T`) | interview, reflection, project narration | intention, memory, perceived meaning
Reception/result (`R`) | feedback, metrics, press, client response | consequence and external interpretation
Context (`C`) | brief, contract, market or cultural reference | conditions and constraints
Interpretation (`I`) | analyst's synthesis | hypothesis only; never evidence

Do not rank final objects as inherently more truthful than process records. Use each source for the kind of claim it can support.

## 2. Record precise provenance

For every selected source, record:

- stable ID;
- project;
- absolute or workspace-resolvable path;
- date and version;
- source role;
- exact page, slide, sheet/range, heading, frame, or timestamp;
- creator and authorship status;
- claim supported;
- limitations.

If a file cannot be read, record it as unavailable. Never infer its contents from the filename.

## 3. Reconstruct projects before comparing them

Complete the project dossier without reference to an intended portfolio narrative. Keep:

- what is explicitly documented;
- what is remembered;
- what is inferred;
- what is missing;

in separate fields. This prevents the later curatorial thesis from rewriting project history.

## 4. Open-code the corpus

Begin with concrete phrases close to the evidence:

- verbs: translates, negotiates, prototypes, systematizes, repairs;
- objects/media: visual identity, paper structure, campaign, installation;
- conditions: limited budget, unclear brief, multi-party approval;
- decisions: simplified, reframed, combined, refused;
- values: clarity, tactility, accessibility, consistency;
- relations: leads, coordinates, supports, learns from;
- effects: adoption, completion, audience response, reusable system.

Avoid generic codes such as “creative,” “good communicator,” or “strategic” until evidence specifies what those labels mean.

## 5. Consolidate a codebook

Merge synonyms only after checking their contexts. For each code, store:

- definition;
- inclusion and exclusion rules;
- source IDs;
- project count;
- positive and counterexamples;
- confidence.

Promote codes into themes through relational reasoning, not frequency alone. Ask:

- What problem connects these actions?
- What changes from one project to the next?
- What tension makes the pattern consequential?
- Does the pattern survive a counterexample?

## 6. Build constellations

A useful constellation contains:

- a recurring question or tension;
- at least two projects, unless one is a documented turning point;
- several source roles when available;
- both continuity and variation;
- an explanation of why the relation matters.

Do not use departments, software lists, or media types as themes unless the case is specifically about them.

## 7. Generate oral-history questions

Ask only questions linked to a known gap:

- “The final deck shows route B, while the early plan emphasizes route A. What changed, and who made that decision?”
- “Two files assign different roles to you. Which tasks did you personally own?”
- “This method recurs in three projects. Was it deliberate at the time or visible only in retrospect?”
- “What evidence would make this interpretation wrong?”

Avoid prompts such as “Tell me about your creative philosophy,” which invite retrospective branding before the facts are stable.
